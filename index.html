Methods

Animal cohorts and sampled tissues

To examine N-glycosylation across organs and ages, we analyzed several mouse cohorts. Brain, spleen, liver, kidney, and plasma came from 54 male C57BL/6JN mice sampled at nine ages from 1 to 28 months (six per age). Purified myelin came from separate cohorts: 18 males at 3, 16, and 29 months (six per age) and 12 females at 3, 13, and 27 months (five, three, and four mice, respectively). These are the datasets you can currently explore in this atlas.

Glycoproteomics and proteomics workflows

To assess N-glycosylation at individual glycosites, we first isolated proteins from each sample. We immobilized the proteins on magnetic beads and digested them with trypsin. Some of the resulting peptides were used for tandem mass tag (TMT) proteomics; from the rest, we enriched glycopeptides with phenylboronic acid beads and analyzed them by LC–MS/MS. We searched the spectra with MSFragger, then applied additional quality control: we retained glycan assignments with q < 0.05, unique peptide backbones, noncontaminant proteins, and an unambiguous match to one glycosite on one protein accession.

Glycosite coverage tiers

Coverage varies widely between glycosites, so we show how much evidence supports each one. For each organ, we add a site’s glycoPSMs across all 54 samples and use that total to assign a coverage tier from 0 to 7. Tier 0 means fewer than five glycoPSMs in the entire organ dataset; tier 7 means at least 1,080. The tiers describe sampling depth, not the proportion of animals in which the site was detected.

Reducing glycan complexity to glycan types

Exact N-glycans are remarkably heterogeneous, so we group their compositions into eight glycan types: stubs, paucimannose, high mannose, phospho, undecorated, fucosylated, sialylated, and fucosylated & sialylated. These broader groups let us compare glycosites across samples, organs, and ages.

Glycan type assignment logic

The eight types let us compare glycans with similar composition and size, including features that may be relevant to their function. The decision tree turns those broader similarities into consistent rules: every exact N-glycan follows the same path and is assigned to one type, even if it has features associated with more than one group.

Glycosite heterogeneity calculations

Two sites can have the same number of glycan types but very different distributions. Shannon entropy (H) measures how evenly the glycoPSMs are divided among those types. We express the result as weighted glycan types, exp(H). This value is 1 if every glycoPSM belongs to one type and equals the observed type count if all types are equally abundant. In the example, the 70%, 10%, 10%, 10% mix has H = 0.94 and 2.56 weighted glycan types; the even 25% mix has H = 1.39 and 4.00 weighted glycan types. We calculate these values for individual samples or from glycoPSMs pooled across samples.

Quantifying differences in glycosite composition

Total variation distance (TVD) compares the proportions of the eight glycan types in two compositions. It is 0% when the compositions are identical and 100% when they have no types in common. The example sites differ by 45%. For pooled site comparisons, we add glycoPSM counts before calculating the proportions. For age group comparisons, we calculate each animal’s composition first, then average across animals in which the site was detected. This gives each measured animal equal weight, regardless of its glycoPSM depth.

Site level composition and summary metrics

For each sample and glycosite, we count the glycoPSMs supporting each exact N-glycan, then add those counts by glycan type. A type’s percent abundance is its share of all retained glycoPSMs at the site. A type is observed if at least one glycoPSM supports it; the type with the largest share is dominant. We use these proportions to describe heterogeneity within a site and composition differences between sites. In the example, Site A is 70% high mannose, Site B has four equally abundant types, and their composition difference is 45%. If a site was not detected in a sample, we record it as missing rather than zero.

Testing Age Related Glycosite Composition

Glycan composition varies from animal to animal, even at the same glycosite. We want to find changes associated with age that stand out from that variation. To test whether a glycosite changes with age, we calculate glycan type percent abundance for each animal. We then average the measured animals in the young and old groups, giving each animal equal weight. The top bars show each animal’s composition; the middle bars show the group means. Total variation distance (TVD) summarizes the difference between those means, from 0% for identical compositions to 100% when they share no glycan types.

To assess whether the observed TVD stands out from variation among animals, we shuffle the young and old labels while keeping the group sizes fixed. We recalculate the group means and TVD after each shuffle. The bottom histogram shows the TVDs produced by these shuffles, and the blue line marks the observed TVD. If k of B shuffles produce a TVD at least as large as the observed one, p = (k + 1)/(B + 1). The p value’s resolution is limited by the shuffles performed and the label assignments the animals permit. With 18 animals in each group, there are 36 choose 18 (approximately 9.1 billion) possible assignments; with 99,999,999 shuffles, the smallest reportable p value is 10⁻⁸.

Lamc1 N574 shows a consistent shift toward undecorated glycans with age (TVD 18.61%, p = 7 × 10⁻⁸).

Nfasc N1047 has different young and old group means (TVD 12.77%), but this observed difference falls within the range produced by shuffled labels and is therefore not significant (p = 0.125).

Thy1 N94 has nearly identical group means (TVD 0.56%, p = 0.675).
